New: avoiding risky hosts beat every migration rule · exploratory

Move the right VMsbefore they fail.

A cloud can live-migrate VMs off a server that is about to fail, but every move costs bandwidth and a pause, and the budget is finite. This project asks which VMs are worth moving: a policy that weighs failure probability, time to failure, criticality and migration cost, against the habit of moving anything above a probability threshold.

01 · How it works

Four signals, one decision. Each signal comes from a model or a real dataset. The cost-sensitive policy uses all four; the threshold baseline uses only the first, which is the comparison this project is about.

STEP 1

Failure probability

A calibrated hazard model turns drive model and age into P(fail within {{ lookahead_days }} days).

HistGBM · Backblaze
STEP 2

Time to failure

A survival curve gives the expected time left, which sets how urgent a move is.

survival curve · urgency weight
STEP 3

Migration cost

Clark et al.'s pre-copy model prices a live migration from the VM's real memory and CPU profile.

pre-copy · Azure VM sizes
STEP 4

Decision

Migrate if the expected cost of waiting (probability × criticality × urgency) beats the migration cost.

v2 re-examined the −3.1% at ρ = 0.

It was mostly a counting effect, not a weakness of cost-awareness: the threshold got 8% more migrations, and every migration was credited with failures a median 170 days later, while the decision used a 30-day probability. With the budget matched and credit limited to the decision's own window, the cost-sensitive rule is ahead by about 0.2%. The v2 theory also predicts no break-even near ρ ≈ 0.3 (see below). 04 · Models

Does a deep attention model beat a calibrated tree model? Both scored on the same held-out test rows. The gradient-boosted hazard model wins on every metric, most clearly on calibration, which is what a decision rule needs from a probability.

05 · v2 research

Turning a course project into research. A 60-week plan (Oct 2026 to Nov 2027) asks whether the idea holds up under real data, a validated simulator, theory and pre-registered statistics. Everything below is work in progress; each number carries where it comes from.

Week 18 benchmark done · Weeks 19–21 runs queued

Data, failure model, real-trace measurement, a validated simulator, policies, theory and a frozen protocol are done, and the first pre-registered benchmark runs (regimes A and E) are in. The code for Weeks 19–28 (the remaining runs, paper draft, artifact and console) is written and tested; the remaining runs take about ten hours of compute.

Budget-aware migration beats doing nothing

Week 18, 30 seeds per cell: BAM saves 5–11% against never migrating in every A regime. The industry-style threshold (P2) costs 2–4× more than doing nothing, so "beating P2", the registered test, says little on its own.

RESULTS_W18.md · ADR-005
Simulated
σκ drives it

The theory's direction holds in simulation

At zero correlation, BAM goes from −2.4% to +1.6% to +5.3% against never migrating as the spread of VM costs rises (σ = 0.25, 0.5, 1.0): the first simulation evidence for the theory's claim.

RESULTS_W18.md · regime E
Exploratory · 30 seeds
+11% to +20%

Where new VMs go matters more than which to move

Keeping new VMs off the riskiest 2% of hosts, with no migrations at all, beats every migration policy tested, in every regime and every seed. It is why the Narya-inspired policy (P5) beats BAM; adding migration on top gains at most about 2 points.

RESULTS_AVOID.md · ADR-010
Simulated · pre-registered
−30% to −51%

The safety guard, as registered, does harm

It ignored pure noise in the smoke test (0 of 5 switches), but its fallback is the threshold P2, so in Week 18 it costs more than never migrating. With a do-nothing fallback it saves 3–9% in regime A (exploratory).
