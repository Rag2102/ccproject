\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cloud operators predict host hardware failures and move virtual machines (VMs) off hosts that are
likely to fail before they do~\cite{levy2020narya,xu2018cdef}. Live migration is not free: each
move consumes network and host capacity and briefly pauses the VM~\cite{clark2005live}, so
operators cap how much they move per day. Under such a cap the question is no longer \emph{whether}
a host is risky but \emph{which} VMs to move first.

The operational default ranks by failure probability alone: migrate VMs on hosts whose predicted
probability exceeds a tuned threshold. A natural alternative is to rank VMs by expected benefit
per unit of budget, so that a small, critical VM on a moderately risky host can go before a large,
unimportant one on a riskier host. Expected-cost decision rules of this kind are
classical~\cite{elkan2001foundations}, and production systems already use cost-aware and learned
mitigation policies~\cite{levy2020narya,wang2022nenya,zhang2024deoxys}. What is missing is an
answer to \emph{when} workload-aware ranking beats a well-tuned threshold, by how much, and
whether the conditions under which it wins occur in real placement. Production results are
reported only as relative improvements on private data~\cite{levy2020narya}, against a fixed
action plan rather than a tuned threshold, and with no public artifact.

Our own course prototype (Section~\ref{sec:v1}) gave a sharp, wrong answer. It found the
cost-aware rule \VVOneAdvRandom\% \emph{behind} a matched threshold when VM criticality and host
risk were independent, and ahead only when they were strongly correlated, suggesting a break-even
correlation. Diagnosing that result is where this paper starts. The loss came from two accounting
errors, not from the decision rule: the threshold received a larger budget, and migrations were
credited with failures a median of \VVOneMedianLeadDays{} days after a 30-day decision. With both
repaired, the same rule is \VVOneAdvAligned\% ahead at zero correlation.

This paper replaces the prototype's sweep with a measurement, a theory and a pre-registered
evaluation. We make four contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A measurement of where real placement sits} (C1, Section~\ref{sec:rho}). In three
  Google 2019 cells, the rank correlation between task criticality and machine risk is
  $|\tau_b| \le \VGOneMaxAbsTauB$ under every tier mapping and risk proxy we tried; a fourth
  cell analysed separately gives $\tau_b = \VSecondTauB$. Critical work is essentially uncorrelated
  with machine risk. The sign depends on analysis choices; the magnitude does not.

  \item \textbf{A break-even theory} (C2, Section~\ref{sec:approach}). Under calibrated
  probabilities, equal budgets and accounting over the decision's own horizon, budget-aware
  ranking never loses in expectation to a threshold on probability. Under a Gaussian copula the
  advantage has a closed form, confirmed by Monte Carlo at all \VTheoryPoints{} points of our
  grid. It is driven by the \emph{spread} of costs per unit of budget and is largest near zero
  correlation; there is no break-even correlation. Noise in the probabilities alone does not
  reverse it, but flattening miscalibration can.

  \item \textbf{A budget-aware policy with a guard, evaluated against a pre-registered bar}
  (C3, Sections~\ref{sec:approach} and~\ref{sec:eval}). Budget-aware migration (BAM) ranks VMs
  by benefit per GB moved. A guard (P6) runs the threshold unless a sequential test and a
  calibration audit both support switching. In a simulator validated against closed forms and the
  prototype, with the threshold tuned to use the same budget, BAM's adjusted advantage in the
  measured regime is \VDeltaPFourBOneTwo\% at a 2\% budget (simultaneous 95\% CI
  [\VCILoPFourBOneTwo, \VCIHiPFourBOneTwo]\%); \VWins{} of \VFamilySize{} confirmatory comparisons
  clear the bar fixed before any evaluation run (an advantage of at least 1\% and Holm-adjusted
  $p < 0.05$).

  \item \textbf{An open benchmark} (C4, Section~\ref{sec:eval}). VMMBench packages hashed failure
  worlds built only from public data, scenario cards, a result schema with a validator, and a
  one-command reduced reproduction that runs in minutes on a laptop.
\end{itemize}
