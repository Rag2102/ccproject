\section{Discussion and threats to validity}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
Three findings do not depend on the simulator. Critical work in the measured Google cells is
essentially uncorrelated with machine risk (Section~\ref{sec:rho}). Under calibrated
probabilities, equal budgets and horizon-matched accounting, budget-aware ranking cannot lose in
expectation, and its advantage grows with the spread of cost per GB rather than with correlation
(Section~\ref{sec:approach}). And the prototype's apparent loss was an accounting artifact
(Section~\ref{sec:v1}). The size of the advantage in a realistic cluster, by contrast, is a
simulator result: it rests on the elements in Table~\ref{tab:assumed}, and we report it as such.

\paragraph{The comparator.}
We tune the threshold to use the budget, because an operator who has been given a budget usually
spends it; Section~\ref{sec:eval} shows the never-migrate and cost-aware-threshold policies beside it
so the reader can see what that choice costs. A threshold tuned on total cost instead would stop
early and narrow the gap, since part of BAM's advantage comes from its stopping rule rather than
its ranking. Narya's own comparator was a fixed action plan~\cite{levy2020narya}; neither choice
is the only reasonable one.

\paragraph{Internal validity.}
The simulator is validated against closed forms, its own predicted failure counts and the
prototype's costs (Table~\ref{tab:g2}), and its policies are compared on common random numbers.
It is still a model. Failures are drawn from drive hazards and an assumed non-disk term, and the
simulated time between a node's failures is less bursty than node logs suggest; correlated
failures across racks are not modelled. The theory assumes a single decision with a fixed
completion probability, while the simulator decides daily with queue-aware completion; the
residual this creates is reported, not tuned away. The threshold and the guard's parameters were
tuned on development seeds disjoint from the evaluation seeds, and the analysis was frozen before
any evaluation run; every later change is logged in the artifact.

\paragraph{External validity.}
The failure data come from one operator's drives, and drives are not hosts. The placement
measurement covers four cells of one provider's 2019 trace, chosen before reading them; it says
nothing about how other schedulers place critical work, and a provider that deliberately puts
critical VMs on new hardware would sit elsewhere on the correlation axis. VM sizes, lifetimes and
tiers come from Azure's 2017 trace; the tier-to-cost mapping is an assumption carried over from
the prototype and is varied. None of the results is a claim about production performance.

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
Three constructs are proxies. A drive failure stands for a host failure. A long machine removal in
the Google trace stands for elevated hardware risk; it also captures decommissioning and
maintenance, which is why we report three proxies, two weightings and three tier mappings and show that the
conclusion holds under each.
And customer impact is measured in tier-weighted minutes of interruption, which ignores that some
interruptions cost far more than their duration.

\paragraph{When to use which policy.}
If the operator's cost per GB varies little across VMs, ranking by probability is enough and
simpler. If it varies widely and the probabilities are well calibrated, budget-aware ranking is
never worse and can be materially better. If calibration cannot be checked, the guard is the safer
choice: it costs little when the evidence is weak because it stays on the threshold.
