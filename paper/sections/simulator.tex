\section{Simulator}
\label{sec:sim}

We evaluate policies in a purpose-built simulator rather than a general cloud simulator, because
the question needs exactly three things modelled faithfully: which VMs are on a host when it fails,
what a migration costs and how long it takes, and a budget shared by all migrations of a day.

\paragraph{Failures.}
A host has $d \in \{1, 4, 12\}$ drives drawn from the Backblaze population with their models and
ages. Its daily hazard is $1 - \prod_k (1 - h_k)\,(1 - h_{\text{other}})$, where $h_k$ is drive $k$'s
hazard and $h_{\text{other}}$ is a Weibull term (shape $0.7$) for non-disk causes, scaled to be
$0\times$, $1\times$ or $3\times$ as likely over a year as disk failures. That multiplier is an
assumption and is swept. Failure times are drawn by inverse transform before any policy runs. A
failed host is repaired after a log-normal time (median two days) and returns empty, with new
drives, to fail again later. Policies can move VMs off a host; they cannot change when it fails.

\paragraph{VMs and placement.}
VMs arrive as a Poisson process and keep the size, CPU utilisation, tier and lifetime of a
resampled Azure VM~\cite{cortez2017resource}. VMs that live at least a day are simulated
individually: they are 12\% of VMs but 92\% of memory-time. Shorter ones are charged at each
host failure as a Poisson draw. No policy may migrate a VM younger than a day, so this
simplification hides nothing a real operator would see, and it costs every policy the same.
Arrivals are placed by the regime under study: uniformly at random, or with a target correlation
between tier and host risk on the latent axis of Section~\ref{sec:rho}.

\paragraph{Migration and accounting.}
A migration is pre-copy~\cite{clark2005live}: memory is sent in rounds until the remainder is
small, then the VM pauses for the last copy. A VM that dirties memory faster than 90\% of the
link does not converge and takes a 60\,s pause. A host's link is shared by its concurrent
migrations, and a failure of the source during a migration interrupts the VM. Costs follow
Section~\ref{sec:problem}: every interruption and every migration that starts is charged, in
customer-impact minutes, and nothing is credited for failures that did not happen. Each day the
policy ranks VMs; the simulator fills the budget in that order, picks each destination as the
less risky of two random hosts with room, and times the migrations.

\paragraph{Common random numbers and time.}
For a given seed, every policy sees the same hosts, failure and repair times, arrivals and
lifetimes. All comparisons are therefore paired. The simulator advances one day at a time and
resolves each day's failures at their exact times against that day's arrivals, departures and
migrations. One policy run of 5{,}000 hosts over 365 days takes 2--5\,s on one laptop core.

\paragraph{Validation.}
Before any policy comparison we required five checks (Table~\ref{tab:g2}). Under a constant
hazard, simulated interruptions match the closed form, both without migration and when every VM
is moved once. Simulated 30-day failure counts match the predicted probabilities across repairs.
Run in our earlier prototype's setting, the simulator's components reproduce that prototype's
two headline costs to within $10^{-6}$ and its migration counts exactly. Property-based tests
check conservation of VMs, capacity, budget and determinism on every simulated day. One check
fails to match the literature. The simulated time between a node's failures has a Weibull shape
near~1, whereas node logs show a more strongly decreasing hazard. We report this as a limitation.
