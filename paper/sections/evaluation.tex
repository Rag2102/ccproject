\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Setup.}
\VHosts{} hosts, \VHorizonDays{} days after a \VWarmupDays-day warm-up, \VEvalSeeds{} evaluation
seeds, four daily budgets (0.5, 1, 2 and 5\% of cluster memory), all policies on the same failure
world per seed. The comparator is the threshold tuned to use the budget (P2); the decision rule is
the one registered in advance (mean regression-adjusted $\Delta \geq 1\%$ and Holm-adjusted
$p<0.05$ over \VFamilySize{} confirmatory comparisons). Policies see the true host probabilities (an
oracle predictor), so that differences come from the policy rather than the model; the results
are therefore what the policies achieve under calibrated probabilities, an upper bound for any
real predictor, whose noise, miscalibration and drift are varied separately. The adjustment uses
each seed's realised failures, which are drawn before any policy runs and are identical across
policies on a seed: it removes seed luck, not policy effect.

\paragraph{Where the measured world sits.}
Across the three Google 2019 cells and every mapping and proxy, $|\tau_b| \leq \VGOneMaxAbsTauB$
(G1 class C0).

\paragraph{Synthetic placement and cost spread (A, E).}
% Written 5 Oct 2026 from the Week 18 run (seeds 1-30), before B and C were opened (PROTOCOL v1.0
% section 9). A3 and A5 triggered the SD extension rule (ADR-007): their numbers are provisional
% until seeds 31-50 are run, and are decided against 2.1%.
Against the threshold tuned to spend the budget, every policy that ranks by expected cost wins
by a wide margin (Figure~F4): BAM's adjusted $\Delta$ is \VDeltaPFourAOneTwo\% at $\rho_L=0$ and a
2\% budget, and all twelve confirmatory comparisons in A3 and A5 meet the pre-registered bar.
These wins say little on their own. The tuned threshold costs more than never migrating in every
seed: at $\rho_L=0$ its saving against never migrating is \VSaveVsPZeroPTwoAOneTwo\% at a 2\% budget
and \VSaveVsPZeroPTwoAOneZeroFive\% at 0.5\%, because spending the budget forces it to move VMs whose
expected benefit is negative.

Against never migrating (Figure~F4b), BAM saves \VSaveVsPZeroPFourAOneTwo\% [\VSaveVsPZeroLoPFourAOneTwo,
\VSaveVsPZeroHiPFourAOneTwo] at $\rho_L = 0$, rising to \VSaveVsPZeroPFourAThreeTwo\% at $\rho_L = 0.3$ and
falling to \VSaveVsPZeroPFourASevenTwo\% at $\rho_L = 0.9$, ahead of never migrating in at least
\VBeatsPZeroPFourASixTwo\% of seeds in every A regime. At zero correlation the saving follows the spread
of cost per GB, as the theory predicts: \VSaveVsPZeroPFourEOneTwo\%, \VSaveVsPZeroPFourETwoTwo\% and
\VSaveVsPZeroPFourEThreeTwo\% for $\sigma_\kappa = 0.25$, $0.5$ and $1$, so with little spread BAM is
slightly worse than doing nothing. The budget never binds: BAM uses \VUtilPFourAOneZeroFive\% of the
smallest budget and its saving is the same at every budget, so its advantage comes from its
stopping rule ($b_i > 0$), not from ranking under scarcity.

Two results cut against the method. The Narya-inspired bandit (P5) saves more than BAM in every A
and E cell (\VSaveVsPZeroPFiveAOneTwo\% at $\rho_L = 0$, \VSaveVsPZeroPFiveAThreeTwo\% at $0.3$) with far
fewer moves (\VMigrationsPFiveAOneTwo{} migrations against BAM's \VMigrationsPFourAOneTwo{} at
$\rho_L=0$): BAM's one-shot benefit $b_i$ ignores that moving today forgoes the option to wait, and
the bandit learns from realised outcomes to move less. And the guard (P6), whose fallback is the
tuned threshold, inherits that threshold's cost: its saving against never migrating is
\VSaveVsPZeroPSixAOneTwo\% at $\rho_L=0$ and a 2\% budget. The closed form (dashed in Figure~F4)
reproduces the direction of the advantage over the threshold in $\rho_L$ and in the budget but
under-predicts its level, which is expected once the budget does not bind and the stopping term
dominates (Section~\ref{sec:approach}).
