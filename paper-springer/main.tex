Cloud VMs}

\abstract{Cloud operators move virtual machines (VMs) off hosts that are predicted to fail, under
a daily limit on how much they can migrate. Production systems learn which mitigation to take, on
private data, and report their gains against their own baselines. We ask the question they leave
open: when does it pay to rank VMs by what they run and what moving them costs? On public data
(Backblaze drive telemetry, Google 2019 placements and Azure VM traces) we measure where real
placement sits, derive the advantage of budget-aware ranking in closed form, and evaluate twelve
policies in a validated simulator under a protocol fixed before any evaluation run. Critical work
is essentially uncorrelated with machine risk: the rank correlation is at most \VGOneMaxAbsTauB{} in absolute value. Budget-aware
migration (BAM) wins all \VFamilySize{} pre-registered comparisons against a threshold tuned to the
same budget, and the result replicates on fresh seeds. That bar turns out to be low, because the
tuned threshold costs more than never migrating. Against never migrating, BAM saves 4.0\% in the
measured regime at the assumed migration cost, but its saving reverses to \textminus 17.4\% at the cheap
end of the published cost range. Keeping new VMs off the riskiest hosts, with no migration at all,
saves 11.2\% on fresh seeds. Two new policies follow: BAM that charges the destination's risk,
which removes the reversal, and per-tier host closing, which keeps the saving while rejecting far
fewer VMs. The simulator, protocol and benchmark are open.}

\keywords{virtual machine migration, failure mitigation, cost-sensitive decisions, cloud
computing, VM placement, simulation}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Hardware failures interrupt the virtual machines (VMs) they host, and large operators now predict
those failures days ahead~\cite{levy2020narya,lin2018ming,xu2018cdef}. A prediction is only useful
if something is done with it. The usual response is to live-migrate VMs off the host before it
fails~\cite{clark2005live}, but migration is not free. Each move uses network and host capacity,
pauses the VM briefly, and is wasted whenever the flagged host would not have failed, which is
most of the time. Operators therefore limit how much they migrate per day, and the decision
becomes a question of priority: which VMs should move first?

The operational default ranks by failure probability alone and moves VMs on hosts above a
threshold. The alternative weighs each VM's expected benefit against its cost: a small, critical
VM on a moderately risky host can be worth more than a large batch VM on a riskier one. Rules of
this form are classical~\cite{elkan2001foundations,dantzig1957discrete}, and production systems
already use cost-aware and learned mitigation. Narya~\cite{levy2020narya} learns which action to
take for a flagged node, NENYA~\cite{wang2022nenya} trades mitigation cost against failures with
cascade reinforcement learning, and Deoxys~\cite{zhang2024deoxys} chooses between rebooting and
redeploying an unhealthy node from causal estimates. All three run at one operator, on private
data, and report their results relative to their own baselines. None of them asks when ranking
VMs by workload pays off at all, or compares against a threshold tuned to the same budget.

This paper answers that question on public data, with a simulator and a protocol that anyone can
rerun. Our first version of this project gave a sharp but wrong answer: it found the cost-aware
rule behind a matched threshold unless critical VMs sat on risky hosts, which suggested a
break-even correlation. Diagnosing that result showed two accounting errors, not a weakness of the
rule, and set the agenda for the work reported here.

We make the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A measurement of real placement} (Section~\ref{sec:rho}). In three Google 2019
cells, chosen before any data was read, the rank correlation between task criticality and machine
risk is $|\tau_b| \le \VGOneMaxAbsTauB$ under every tier mapping and risk proxy tested.
\item \textbf{A closed form for when ranking pays} (Section~\ref{sec:method}). Under calibrated
probabilities, equal budgets and horizon-matched accounting, budget-aware ranking never loses in
expectation to a probability threshold. Its advantage is driven by the spread of cost per GB and is
largest near zero correlation; there is no break-even correlation. A Monte Carlo check agrees at
all \VTheoryPoints{} grid points.
\item \textbf{A pre-registered evaluation and an open benchmark} (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}
and~\ref{sec:results}). Twelve policies are compared on paired seeds in a simulator validated
against closed forms. BAM wins all \VFamilySize{} confirmatory comparisons against the tuned
threshold, and we show why that bar is weak.
\item \textbf{The migration-cost condition} (Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}). Across the published
range of live-migration costs, BAM's saving against never migrating runs from $-17.4\%$ to
$+4.0\%$, so the headline holds only under stated costs.
\item \textbf{Placement before migration, and two new policies} (Sections~\ref{sec:res-avoid}
and~\ref{sec:res-new}). Avoiding risky hosts for new VMs beats every migration policy, follows a
simple concentration law, and motivates BAM with destination risk and per-tier host closing, both
confirmed on fresh seeds under rules fixed before the runs.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work and Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the decision
problem. Section~\ref{sec:method} gives the policies and the theory, Sections~\ref{sec:arch}
and~\ref{sec:impl} the system and its implementation, and Section~\ref{sec:setup} the data,
simulator and protocol. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results and compares them with the
base papers, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them and the threats to validity, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Failure prediction in cloud infrastructure}\label{sec:rel-pred}

Field studies of large drive populations established that replacement rates far exceed datasheet
values and that SMART attributes carry real but incomplete signal~\cite{schroeder2007disk}. Later
work predicts drive replacement from SMART data~\cite{botezatu2016predicting}, and production
systems at Microsoft rank disks by error-proneness from SMART and system signals with a
cost-sensitive loss, acting on the top few under capacity constraints~\cite{xu2018cdef}. MING
predicts node failures by combining an LSTM over temporal signals, a random forest over spatial
signals and a ranking model, with a cost-sensitive function to choose the cut-off; its authors
note that predicting faulty nodes enables VMs to be allocated and migrated to healthy
ones~\cite{lin2018ming}. NTAM extends disk failure prediction with the status of neighbouring
disks and an attention-based temporal component, and is deployed on Azure and Microsoft
365~\cite{luo2021ntam}. Memory is another major cause: HiMFP predicts uncorrectable memory errors
from correctable-error patterns and reports about 45\% fewer VM interruptions from such
errors~\cite{yu2023himfp}.

Recent journal work broadens the toolset. Tengku Asmawi et al.\ compare five traditional and three
deep learning models for job and task failure on Google cluster traces, finding gradient boosting
best for jobs and tree models best for tasks~\cite{asmawi2022cloud}. On the Backblaze data used
here, an explainable XGBoost model over SMART time-series features reaches a 74.7\% detection rate
at a 0.73\% false-alarm rate~\cite{li2025explainable}, and a knowledge-graph approach adds context
such as how failures relate to one another~\cite{chhetri2022kg}. GQAT-Net adds a calibrated
attention architecture for failures in cluster traces~\cite{tripathi2025gqat}.

All of these stop at the prediction, or name migration as a downstream use without deciding which
VMs to move. We use a predictor of this kind as an input and do not claim a better one. Instead we
ask what a decision built on its probabilities needs from it.

\subsection{Proactive failure mitigation}\label{sec:rel-mitig}

Narya~\cite{levy2020narya} flags nodes with rules and a gradient-boosted model, then chooses among
composite actions (mark unallocatable, live-migrate, wait, send to repair) with A/B tests and a
Thompson-sampling bandit, learning from interruptions observed over the following weeks. It
reduced VM interruptions by about 26\% against the previous fixed action plan. NENYA~\cite{wang2022nenya}
mitigates failures of Microsoft 365 database copies with cascade reinforcement learning, whose
cost is fixed per action type. Deoxys~\cite{zhang2024deoxys} acts after a node turns unhealthy and
chooses between Reboot and Redeploy from double machine learning with a causal forest, reporting
53\% less VM downtime than the legacy policy in an A/B test. Protean~\cite{hadary2020protean}
allocates VMs at scale and can avoid unhealthy hosts at allocation time.

A second line of work treats proactive fault tolerance as an allocation problem. VMFT-LAD detects
VM failures early from logs with a Matrix Profile and large language models, so that VMs can be
migrated before the failure~\cite{senevirathne2024vmft}. Ramesh et al.\ formulate the allocation of
VMs from deteriorating hosts as an integer linear program that minimises migration time, solved
with hybrid genetic and simulated-annealing heuristics~\cite{ramesh2024ilp}. These methods decide
where and how fast to move VMs once a host is suspect; none of them ranks VMs by their criticality
and cost under a shared migration budget, or asks when doing so beats a probability threshold.

\subsection{Live migration cost}\label{sec:rel-mig}

Pre-copy live migration sends memory in rounds while the VM runs, then pauses it for a final
copy~\cite{clark2005live}. Its cost depends on the link, the VM's memory and how fast the workload
dirties pages. Measurements span a wide range: from tens of milliseconds of downtime for
interactive servers on Xen~\cite{clark2005live} to several seconds for a loaded web
application~\cite{voorsluys2009cost}, with effective throughput from about a tenth of the link~\cite{clark2005live} to about three
quarters, depending on the hypervisor~\cite{akoush2010predicting,hu2013quantitative}. Elsaid et al.\ survey
the models that predict these costs~\cite{elsaid2021survey}, and Haris et al.\ use a learned model
to stop pre-copy early when predicted downtime is low enough, cutting downtime on a KVM
testbed~\cite{haris2024precopy}. Virt-LM benchmarks migration mechanisms
themselves~\cite{huang2011virtlm}. We do not improve migration; we take its cost as an input and
sweep it across this published range.

\subsection{VM placement and cluster traces}\label{sec:rel-place}

Public traces make realistic inputs available. Resource Central characterises Azure VM workloads
and shows that most VMs are short-lived while a long-lived minority holds most
memory-time~\cite{cortez2017resource}. The 2019 Borg traces record placements, priorities and
machine events for eight Google cells~\cite{tirmazi2020borg}. Recent work reschedules VMs with deep
reinforcement learning on production traces~\cite{ding2025vm2rl}. Schedulers place work for
packing, priority and spread, not for hardware risk, so there is no reason to expect critical work
to sit on safer or riskier machines, but we found no published measurement of it.

\subsection{Cost-sensitive decisions, simulators and benchmarks}\label{sec:rel-bench}

Acting when expected cost exceeds a threshold is the classical cost-sensitive
rule~\cite{elkan2001foundations}, and filling a budget by benefit density is the greedy solution
of the knapsack relaxation~\cite{dantzig1957discrete}. We claim neither. General cloud simulators
such as CloudSim Plus~\cite{cloudsimplus} and OpenDC~\cite{opendc} model faults and migration, and
platforms such as Park~\cite{mao2019park}, Gymnasium~\cite{towers2024gymnasium} and
DCcluster-Opt~\cite{guillenperez2025dccluster} standardise environments for learned policies. None
combines calibrated failure worlds built from public data, a migration budget shared by every
move, paired comparisons on common random numbers and a frozen protocol. Following
holistic-evaluation practice~\cite{liang2023helm}, our benchmark documents every scenario with a
card and traces every number to a file.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

A cluster of hosts runs VMs. A host fails when any of its components fails, and every VM on it is
interrupted. Once a day the operator may live-migrate some VMs to other hosts; each migration uses
part of a daily budget $B$, measured in GB of memory moved. Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the
notation.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering
\caption{Notation.}\label{tab:notation}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{ll}\toprule
Symbol & Meaning \\ \midrule
$j$, $i$ & host; VM, on host $j(i)$ \\
$H$ & risk horizon in days (30 unless stated) \\
$p_j$ & probability that host $j$ fails within $H$, given that it is up now \\
$c_i$ & cost if VM $i$ is interrupted: tier weight times recovery downtime, weighted by \\
 & the probability that $i$ is still running at the failure \\
$e_i$ & probability that $i$'s migration finishes before its host fails \\
$m_i$ & cost of migrating $i$ (pause and resource time, in the units of $c_i$) \\
$w_i$ & memory of $i$ in GB, the amount it uses from the budget \\
$B$ & daily budget in GB \\
$\kappa_i$ & averted cost per unit of budget, $c_i e_i / w_i$ \\
$\rho$, $\rho_L$ & correlation of $\log\kappa$ and $\log p$; $\rho_L$ on the latent copula axis \\
$\sigma_p$, $\sigma_\kappa$ & standard deviations of $\log p$ and $\log\kappa$ \\
$q$ & share of VMs the budget allows \\
$\Delta$ & advantage over the threshold, $(C_{P2}-C)/C_{P2}$ \\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{definition}[Expected benefit of moving a VM]\label{def:benefit}
Migrating VM $i$ changes the expected cost by
\begin{equation}
b_i = p_{j(i)}\, c_i\, e_i - m_i ,
\label{eq:benefit}
\end{equation}
the expected interruption cost avoided minus the cost of moving.
\end{definition}

Each day the operator solves
\begin{equation}
\max_x \sum_i b_i x_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_i w_i x_i \le B, \quad x_i \in \{0,1\},
\label{eq:knapsack}
\end{equation}
and the expected cost of moving a set $S$ is
\begin{equation}
C(S) = C_0 - \sum_{i \in S} b_i, \qquad C_0 = \sum_i p_{j(i)}\, c_i .
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
Problem~\eqref{eq:knapsack} is a 0--1 knapsack. Its fractional relaxation is solved exactly by
ranking VMs by the density $b_i / w_i$ and filling the budget while $b_i > 0$, and the integer
problem differs from it by at most one item~\cite{dantzig1957discrete}.

The operational baseline migrates VMs on hosts with $p_j \ge \tau$. At an equal budget it is the
special case of~\eqref{eq:knapsack} that ranks by $p_j$ and ignores $c$, $m$, $e$ and $w$. We
compare policies by the advantage
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \frac{C_{P2} - C_{\mathrm{BAM}}}{C_{P2}},
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
and, because a tuned threshold can be worse than doing nothing, also by the saving against never
migrating, $S = (C_{P0} - C)/C_{P0}$.

Policies are compared by the cost that actually occurs in simulation: the interruption cost of
every VM on a host when it fails, plus the cost of every migration. No migration is credited with
a failure it averted, and the horizon $H$ of the probability a policy uses is the horizon over
which its consequences are observed. Our earlier prototype broke this rule: it credited
migrations with failures a median of \VVOneMedianLeadDays{} days after a 30-day decision, and gave
the threshold \VVOneExtraGB\% more memory to move. Those two errors, not the decision rule,
explained its apparent \VVOneAdvRandom\% loss; repaired, the same rule is \VVOneAdvAligned\% ahead
at zero correlation.

\subsection{Budget-aware migration}\label{sec:bam}

BAM (P4) ranks VMs by density $b_i/w_i$ and moves them while $b_i > 0$ and budget remains
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:bam}). Two details matter in practice. The interruption cost $c_i$ is weighted
by the probability that the VM is still running when its host would fail, since most VMs leave
long before then. And $e_i$ depends on the VM's place in its host's migration queue,
$e_i = \exp(-\lambda_j t)$ with $\lambda_j = -\ln(1-p_j)/H$ and $t$ the queue ahead of $i$ plus its
own migration time, so it is recomputed after every pick with a lazy max-heap. On simulator
snapshots the greedy is within \VBamExactGap\% of an exact solver on average.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Budget-aware migration (BAM, P4), one daily decision}\label{alg:bam}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require eligible VMs with $p_j, c_i, m_i, w_i$; daily budget $B$
\State compute $e_i$ from the migration queue on each host and $b_i$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:benefit}
\State put every VM in a max-heap keyed by $b_i / w_i$; $S \gets \emptyset$; $U \gets 0$
\While{the heap is not empty}
  \State pop VM $i$; if its host's queue changed, recompute $e_i$ and $b_i$ and reinsert it
  \If{$b_i \le 0$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \If{$U + w_i \le B$ and a destination has capacity}
    \State $S \gets S \cup \{i\}$; $U \gets U + w_i$; reserve the destination
  \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State \Return $S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{When ranking pays: a closed form}\label{sec:theory}

Under four assumptions (calibrated $p$, failures independent given $p$, the same budget for every
policy, additive costs) and accounting over the horizon the decision used, BAM is never worse in
expectation than a threshold on $p$ with the same budget, because the threshold is one feasible
solution of~\eqref{eq:knapsack}.

\begin{proposition}[Ranking term]\label{prop:rank}
Let $(\log p, \log \kappa)$ follow a Gaussian copula with correlation $\rho$, let the budget allow a
share $q$ of VMs with $z_q = \Phi^{-1}(q)$, and let
$\sigma_z^2 = \sigma_p^2 + 2\rho\sigma_p\sigma_\kappa + \sigma_\kappa^2$. When the budget binds for
both rules, the advantage of density ranking over probability ranking has main term
\begin{equation}
\Phi(z_q + \sigma_z) - \Phi(z_q + \sigma_p + \rho\,\sigma_\kappa) \;\ge\; 0 .
\label{eq:rankterm}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

The term is non-negative because
$\sigma_z^2 - (\sigma_p + \rho\sigma_\kappa)^2 = \sigma_\kappa^2 (1-\rho^2) \ge 0$, and it is zero
only when costs do not vary ($\sigma_\kappa = 0$) or are perfectly aligned with risk
($|\rho| = 1$). The advantage is therefore driven by the spread of cost per GB and is largest near
$\rho = 0$, not above a break-even correlation. The same analysis gives two cautions. Noise in $p$
alone does not make BAM lose unless costs are anti-aligned with risk, but flattening
miscalibration ($p^\gamma$ with $\gamma < 1$) can, because the stopping rule $b_i > 0$ then compares a
distorted $p\,c$ with $m$. A Monte Carlo check agrees with the closed form at all
\VTheoryPoints{} grid points (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}).

\subsection{Baselines and the guard}\label{sec:baselines}

The threshold P2 is tuned on development seeds, separately for each regime and budget: the
threshold and the order in which a host's VMs move are chosen so that it uses its budget, as an
operator who has been given a budget usually does. P5 is a Narya-inspired policy with three
actions (do nothing, live-migrate, avoid allocation), chosen per risk bucket by Thompson sampling;
every difference from the paper is recorded in the artifact. The guard P6 runs BAM only when a
bootstrap lower bound on BAM's model-based advantage over P2 is positive and a calibration audit
passes at two consecutive looks, and otherwise falls back to P2, as registered.

\subsection{Placement and two new policies}\label{sec:new}

The evaluation showed that the Narya-inspired policy beat BAM, and diagnosing why pointed to its
avoid-allocation action. P0+avoid isolates that action: it closes the riskiest $q$ of up hosts to
new VMs and never migrates. A VM drawn onto a closed host is placed elsewhere. A second diagnosis,
of why BAM collapses when migration is cheap, led to two further policies. BAM's benefit assumes
that a moved VM is safe; P4-net charges the destination's risk,
$b_i = (p_j e_i - p_{\mathrm{dst}})\,c_i - m_i$, where $p_{\mathrm{dst}}$ is the expected risk of
the host the engine's destination rule delivers. Closing a host to every VM also rejects VMs when
capacity is tight; P0+selective closes the riskiest $q_t$ of hosts only to tier $t$, with
$q = (2\%, 5\%, 10\%)$ for low, medium and high criticality, so that a risky host stays open to
less critical work. Neither policy migrates more than BAM; P0+selective does not migrate at all.

\section{System Architecture}\label{sec:arch}

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. Public data feed a failure model whose calibrated
hazards define \emph{failure worlds}: for each seed, every host's drives, failure and repair times,
and every VM's arrival, size, tier and lifetime are drawn once and hashed, so every policy faces
exactly the same world. The simulator advances one day at a time. Each day the policy under test
proposes moves; the engine fills the shared GB budget in that order, places each VM on the less
risky of two random hosts with room, and times the migrations. Results from every policy, regime,
budget and seed are written as validated records and analysed with paired statistics. The same
records feed the paper's tables, its figures and a read-only results viewer, and the benchmark
packages the worlds, scenario cards and validator so that other policies can be scored on the
same terms.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}

The system is written in Python (Table~\ref{tab:stack}) and is available at \repourl. The
simulator lives in \texttt{vmsim/}: failure model, placement, migration timing, cost accounting
and one module per policy. \texttt{vmmbench/} holds the result and scenario-card schemas and a
validator. \texttt{scripts/v2/} contains every step from raw data to the paper's numbers, in
numbered order, and \texttt{configs/v2/} the frozen experiment matrix. Every number in this paper
is generated from \texttt{results/summary/} by a script, never typed into a table by hand.

Runs are sharded across local cores with joblib; one policy run of 5{,}000 hosts over a year takes
2--5\,s on a laptop core. The test suite of 149 tests includes property-based tests with
Hypothesis that check, on every simulated day, conservation of VMs, memory accounting, capacity,
the budget and determinism. A one-command reduced reproduction (\texttt{make reproduce-small})
runs the pipeline end to end in a few minutes.

\paragraph{Drive failures.} Backblaze publishes one record per drive per day with the drive's
model, capacity, a failure flag and its SMART attributes. We converted all 45 published archives
(35.1\,GB compressed) to columnar files, checked every archive's row count against its CSV files
and recorded a checksum for every file. The data have no duplicate drive-days and no invalid
failure flags; a drive fails at its first failure record, later records are dropped, and a drive
absent on a given day is not counted as failed. A drive stands in for a host component; a host's
failure probability is composed from its drives and an assumed non-disk term
(Section~\ref{sec:sim}).

\paragraph{Failure model.}\label{sec:model} We predict whether a drive fails within $H$ days from
its SMART attributes, their trends, its model and its age, training only on the past: training
ends in August 2025, validation (used to choose the model and fit its calibration) in January
2026, and the test period runs to June 2026. At $H = 30$ a gradient-boosted model reaches a test
PR-AUC of \VPrAucSMART{} and ROC-AUC of \VRocAucSMART; the first version's features (model,
capacity, age) refitted on the same split reach only \VPrAucVOneFeatures, barely above the base
rate. A logistic regression on the same features does as well (\VPrAucLogit), so the signal is in
the SMART telemetry rather than the model class. A rule fixed in advance, the highest validation
PR-AUC, selected a 20-member bootstrap ensemble (\VPrAucEnsembleVal{} against \VPrAucLogitVal{}
for the next best), and isotonic calibration on the validation period makes its probabilities
usable as $p$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:benefit}.

\paragraph{Where real placement sits.}\label{sec:rho} For three Google 2019 cells we compute
Kendall's $\tau_b$ between the criticality tier of every placement and the risk proxy of its
machine, over three weekly windows after a warm-up week, with bias-corrected bootstrap intervals
over machines. The trace has no hardware-failure labels, so the risk proxy is a machine platform's
rate of \emph{long} removals, those not followed by re-addition within 24 hours. The cells, the
tier mapping, the proxies and the decision rule were fixed before any data was read. In every cell,
under every combination of three mappings, three proxies and two weightings,
$|\tau_b| \le \VGOneMaxAbsTauB$, and the upper end of every latent interval is at most
\VGOneMaxLatentUpper{} (Figure~\ref{fig:rho}). The sign changes with the analysis choices; the
magnitude does not. A fourth cell, chosen by a rule fixed before it was downloaded, gives
$\tau_b = \VSecondTauB$. Critical work is essentially uncorrelated with machine risk, so the
realistic regimes of the evaluation place VMs at these measured values.

cells (a, b, h), on the latent copula axis with 95\% intervals, for each tier mapping, risk proxy

\subsection{Simulator and validation}\label{sec:sim}

A host has several drives drawn from the Backblaze population with their models and ages. Its
daily hazard is $1 - \prod_k (1-h_k)(1-h_{\mathrm{other}})$, where $h_k$ is drive $k$'s hazard and
$h_{\mathrm{other}}$ is an assumed Weibull term for non-disk causes. Failure times are drawn before
any policy runs; a failed host is repaired after a log-normal time (median two days) and returns
empty. VMs arrive as a Poisson process and keep the size, tier and lifetime of a resampled Azure
VM. VMs that live at least a day are simulated individually (12\% of VMs, 92\% of memory-time);
shorter ones are charged at each host failure as a Poisson draw, and no policy may migrate a VM
younger than a day. Migration is pre-copy with a shared host link, and a source failure during a
migration interrupts the VM. Every policy sees the same hosts, failures, arrivals and lifetimes
for a given seed.

Before any policy comparison, the simulator had to pass five checks (Table~\ref{tab:g2}): analytic
cases under a constant hazard, self-calibration of 30-day failure counts across repairs, a
regression against the first version's two headline costs, property-based invariants, and a run
time target.

\subsection{Regimes, protocol and statistics}\label{sec:protocol}

Regimes differ in how VMs are placed. B1 calibrates placement to the measured correlation and
dispersion, and B2 replays class-conditional placement from the Google machine platforms; these are
the realistic regimes. A1--A7 sweep the latent correlation from 0 to 0.9, E1--E3 sweep the cost
spread at zero correlation, C places critical VMs on the riskiest hosts, and D shifts placement
and degrades the predictor mid-run. Each run has \VHosts{} hosts with four drives each, simulated for
\VHorizonDays{} days after a \VWarmupDays-day warm-up, with $H = 30$ and daily budgets of 0.5, 1, 2
and 5\% of cluster memory. Policies see the true host probabilities (an oracle predictor) in the
main matrix, so gains are an upper bound; noisy predictors are tested separately.

The protocol was frozen before any evaluation run. The confirmatory family is \VFamilySize{}
comparisons of P4, P5 and P6 against P2 in regimes A3, A5, B1, B2 and C at 1\% and 2\%. A
comparison wins if its mean regression-adjusted $\Delta$ is at least 1\% and its Holm-adjusted
one-sided $p < 0.05$. Intervals are bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap intervals over
paired seeds. Seed families never mix: development seeds 101--110 tune P2 and the guard,
evaluation seeds 1--30 run the matrix and were extended to 50 where a pre-registered variance rule
required it, and replication seeds 1001--1050 repeat the family. The full matrix is 528 cells and
19{,}140 runs, with none missing. Every test added after the freeze has its own seed family
(2001--2050, 3001--3050, 4001--4050) and a rule written before its run.

\paragraph{Migration-cost range.} Migration cost is set by the usable share of the link, the rate
at which the workload dirties memory, and the pause. A rule written before any rerun takes, from
four published measurement studies~\cite{clark2005live,voorsluys2009cost,akoush2010predicting,hu2013quantitative},
the extremes of each parameter: a cheap end (77\% usable share, 2.3\,MB/s dirty rate, 0.06\,s
pause) and an expensive end (10\%, 200\,MB/s, 3\,s), against the assumed default (50\%,
150\,MB/s, 1\,s). Each end combines extremes from different papers, so no measured system sits at
either end; the range brackets the cost rather than estimating it.

\subsection{Budget-aware migration against the tuned threshold}\label{sec:res-main}

BAM wins all \VFamilySize{} confirmatory comparisons. In the measured regime B1 at a 2\% budget,
its adjusted advantage over the tuned threshold is \VDeltaPFourBOneTwo\% (95\% interval
[\VCILoPFourBOneTwo, \VCIHiPFourBOneTwo]\%), and every comparison clears the bar with
Holm-adjusted $p \le 3\times10^{-14}$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The replication on seeds 1001--1050
agrees: 30 of 30 win, no comparison changes sign, and the largest gap from the main result is 1.2
points. The advantage falls slowly with the latent correlation and is present in every regime
(Figure~\ref{fig:adv}).

\subsection{Theory against simulation}\label{sec:res-theory}

The closed form matches the Monte Carlo at every grid point (Figure~\ref{fig:theory}). In the
simulator it gets the direction right. At zero correlation BAM's saving against never migrating
rises from $-2.4\%$ to $+1.6\%$ to $+5.3\%$ as the cost spread $\sigma_\kappa$ grows from 0.25 to
0.5 to 1.0 (regimes E1--E3), as Proposition~\ref{prop:rank} predicts, and with little spread BAM
is slightly worse than doing nothing. The closed form under-predicts the level of $\Delta$ by 7 to 24
points, as expected when the budget does not bind and the stopping rule, which the one-shot model
leaves out, dominates.

\subsection{Sensitivity to migration cost}\label{sec:res-cost}

Table~\ref{tab:costrange} reruns BAM and the threshold at the two ends of the published cost range.
BAM's win over P2 holds across the range (by 46\% to 95\%), mostly because P2 does so badly. Its
saving against never migrating does not hold. At the expensive end BAM nearly stops migrating and
saves about 1\% or less. At the cheap end it reverses in all three regimes, to $-17.4\%$ in B1,
with 290{,}000 to 356{,}000 migrations per run. Cheaper migration makes BAM worse: when moving looks
almost free, the benefit $b_i$ is positive for many VMs, and BAM moves the same VMs again and
again. On five development seeds in B1, a version of BAM that may move each VM only once recovers
most of the loss ($-15.8\%$ to $+3.2\%$), and charging destination risk recovers more
($+10.0\%$). The headline saving therefore holds only at stated migration costs.

\subsection{Placement without migration}\label{sec:res-avoid}

Closing the riskiest 2\% of hosts to new VMs, with no migration at all, beats every migration
policy. On fresh seeds 2001--2050, under a rule committed before the run, it saves 11.2\%
[10.3, 12.1] in B1 and 8.9\% in B2 against never migrating, and beats BAM and the Narya-inspired
policy in both regimes: all six comparisons pass (Table~\ref{tab:avoid}). Adding BAM's migrations
on top gains only 0.3 points in B1 for about 15{,}000 migrations a run. The cost is rejected
arrivals, which rise from 169 to 175 a run in B1. Avoidance weakens with noisy predictions, to
3.8\% in B1 at noise $\sigma = 1$ redrawn daily, but stays positive where BAM loses heavily.

The saving follows a simple concentration law, $S = \varphi\, R(q, \sigma)$, where $R$ is the share of
failures that land on the closed hosts and $\varphi$ is the share of interruption cost that placement
can reach. On 19 out-of-sample cells (seeds 3001--3050), the law's predictions rank the observed
savings with Spearman correlation 0.98 in B1 and 0.97 in B2, with mean errors of 1.2 and 0.9
points. Because $\varphi$ is fitted once per regime, this is a regularity, not a derivation.

\subsection{Destination-aware BAM and per-tier closing}\label{sec:res-new}

Both new policies pass every test of their pre-registered families on seeds 4001--4050
(Tables~\ref{tab:net} and~\ref{tab:selective}). Charging destination risk removes BAM's collapse:
in B1 the saving moves from $-17.1\%$ to $+8.1\%$ with cheap migration and from $-77.8\%$ to
$+9.6\%$ at a higher cost level, with an order of magnitude fewer migrations, and it adds
0.85 points at the default costs. It does not fix noise: at $\sigma = 1$, redrawn every 30 days, it
still loses 18.4\% in B1.

Per-tier closing keeps the saving of whole-host closing and rejects far fewer VMs: 66 instead of
280 a run in B1, with a saving of 17.5\% against 16.9\%. The gain in saving is under two points, and
in B1 it cannot be told from zero against a same-mechanism comparator without tiers; the clear gain
is in rejections. With per-tier closing in place, adding destination-aware migration adds nothing
measurable (17.4\% against 17.5\% in B1).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the results mean.} Three findings do not depend on the simulator: real placement is
essentially uncorrelated with machine risk, budget-aware ranking cannot lose in expectation under
the theory's assumptions, and its advantage grows with the spread of cost per GB rather than with
correlation. The size of the gain in a realistic cluster is a simulator result, and it is
conditional. BAM beats a threshold tuned to its budget by a wide margin everywhere, but that
threshold is worse than doing nothing, and BAM's own saving against never migrating depends on
what migration costs. The practical lesson is the one in the title. Choosing where new VMs go
captures more than choosing which VMs to move, does so without migrating, and degrades gently when
predictions are noisy. Migration helps only when its benefit is weighed carefully, including the
risk at the destination.

\paragraph{The comparator.} We tuned the threshold to use its budget because the protocol says so
and because operators given a budget tend to spend it. A threshold tuned on total cost would stop
earlier and narrow the gap, since much of BAM's advantage is its stopping rule. That is why every
results table shows never migrating beside the threshold.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} Table~\ref{tab:assumed} lists every assumed or synthetic element.
Internally, the simulator is validated against closed forms and its own failure counts, but it is
still a model: failures come from drive hazards and an assumed non-disk term, the simulated time
between a node's failures is less bursty than node logs suggest, and correlated failures across
racks are not modelled. The main matrix uses an oracle predictor, so its gains are upper bounds.
Two noise definitions appear: the avoidance test redraws noise daily and the ADR-013 tests every 30
days, so their noise results are not comparable. Externally, the failure data come from one
operator's drives, the placement measurement from one provider's 2019 trace, and migration costs
from four of six planned published sources. For construct validity, a drive failure stands for a
host failure and a long machine removal for elevated risk. Finally, the tests added after the
freeze have blind seeds, but their policies and settings came from development seeds; the
avoidance rule was committed before its run, while the later rules were frozen by a stored hash.

\paragraph{When to use which policy.} If cost per GB varies little across VMs, ranking by
probability is enough. If it varies widely and probabilities are well calibrated, budget-aware
ranking is never worse, provided migration is not so cheap that it invites repeated moves; charging
destination risk guards against that. Where placement can be controlled, closing risky hosts to new
VMs, per tier where capacity is tight, should come first.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked when ranking VMs by expected benefit per GB beats a tuned probability threshold under a
daily migration budget. In real placement, as far as public traces show, critical work is
uncorrelated with machine risk. That does not doom workload awareness: under calibrated
probabilities and horizon-matched accounting, budget-aware ranking cannot lose in expectation, and
its advantage is driven by the spread of cost per GB. In a validated simulator it wins all
\VFamilySize{} pre-registered comparisons, but its saving against never migrating holds only at
stated migration costs, and placement captures more than migration. BAM with destination risk and
per-tier host closing address the two weaknesses we found. Nothing here is a claim about
production performance. The next steps are measured migration costs on a KVM/QEMU testbed,
rack-correlated failures, and opening the benchmark's hidden seeds to outside policies.
